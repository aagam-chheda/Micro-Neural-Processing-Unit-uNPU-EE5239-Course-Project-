00Using this notebook

The failure mode we are guarding against is a document that states 50 MHz and C[m][j] = Σ A[m][k]·B[k][j] in the same voice. One of those is a guess and the other is arithmetic. Three weeks into debugging you need to know instantly which claims you can lean on.

So every substantive claim below carries one of four markers.

Spec

Comes from the course brief or the top-level interface contract. Not ours to change. Where the wording is ambiguous, the ambiguity is quoted rather than resolved, and a matching Open block appears nearby.

Derived

Follows from the spec by arithmetic you can redo yourself. The derivation is shown so it can be audited, not just believed. If you find one of these wrong, it is a bug in the notebook and it should be fixed here first, then in the RTL.

Decided

Our choice. States the alternatives we rejected and why. Revisable by the team — but revise it here, with a line in the decision log (§14), not silently in someone's RTL.

Openowner · due

Nobody has answered this yet. Every one of these has a named owner and a date by which it blocks something. The filter bar at the top of the page will dim everything except these — that view is the agenda for the next instructor meeting.

Colour code for planes

Used consistently in every diagram and table: data plane (activations, results, DMA) · weight plane (stationary operands) · control plane (APB, CSRs, FSMs, handshakes).

House rules

  • Signal names are written exactly as they appear in RTL: dma_ready, not "the ready signal".
  • Cycle numbers are always relative to the first activation injection unless stated otherwise.
  • Anything that is a number gets a unit and a source.
  • If you change a decision, add a dated line to §14. Do not edit the old one away — the reason it changed is usually the useful part.

01What the brief actually says

This section exists so nobody has to go back to the PDF. Everything here is quoted or directly restated from the course document; nothing is inferred.

Our scope

Spec

Front-end. A 4×4 systolic array for INT8 matrix multiplication using a weight-stationary dataflow. A DMA master engine that fetches tensor data from SRAM. Data-skewing FIFO pipelines producing correctly staggered wavefront alignment into the array.

Back-end. Physical design of the uNPU as an independent hard macro: macro-level floorplanning, power grid synthesis, resolving localised routing congestion from the 16 parallel multipliers, and STA focused on setup violations in the PE combinational datapath.

Five named subblocks. APB wrapper and configuration registers; DMA master engine (SRAM fetching FSM); data formatting and skewing FIFOs; PE core (multiplier and accumulator datapath); 2D systolic grid assembly.

Required tests

Spec

Deliverables: functionally validated RTL for the DMA and systolic array, plus a DRC/LVS-clean macro as .lef, .lib and .gds.

Four consequences we have to design around

Derived

1. We are one of three masters on the SRAM. Project 1's arbiter must "prioritize and route requests from multiple bus masters: the RISC-V CPU (Instruction and Data), the uNPU DMA engine, and the SPI Controller." PicoRV32 fetches an instruction almost every cycle and has no cache. So the CPU is a near-continuous bus client, and our DMA competes with it for every beat.

This is not a theoretical stall. It is the normal case, and it is why the buffering in §05 is not optional.

Derived

2. Address spaces do not overlap. The control plane reaches us at 0x4000_0xxx over APB; our DMA only ever emits addresses in 0x0000_0000–0x0000_7FFF. A pointer from one space appearing in the other is always a firmware bug, which is what makes an address-range check in our ERROR path cheap and worthwhile.

Spec

3. The SPI controller can master the internal bus. Project 1's SPI block is a "critical debug interface; if the RISC-V CPU fails to boot in actual silicon, an external host can use the SPI interface to master the internal bus and test the hard macros directly."

So our APB port may be driven by something that is not the CPU, at an arbitrary and probably very slow rate. Our slave logic must not assume back-to-back transfers or any particular timing.

Spec

4. A BIST FSM will exercise this macro. Project 1 builds a "hardware state machine that runs autonomous diagnostic patterns on the SRAM and hard macros to verify manufacturing integrity post-fabrication, completely independent of the software stack."

03The math

3.1 The operation

Spec

Given activations A of shape M×K and weights B of shape K×N, produce C of shape M×N.

C[m][j]  =  Σk=0..K-1   A[m][k] × B[k][j]

m indexes output rows (samples) · j indexes output columns (neurons) · k is the reduction axis.

Derived

Three indices, three loops, and the whole microarchitecture is a decision about where each one goes. In our array: k becomes the vertical axis (the partial sum falls down a column picking up one term per row), j becomes the horizontal axis (four output columns computed side by side), and m stays a loop in time, one iteration per clock.

The array being 4×4 therefore fixes K = 4 and N = 4 per pass. M is unbounded. That asymmetry drives everything in §08.

3.2 Why weight-stationary

Spec

The brief mandates it. This section records why, because the reasoning determines what we optimise later.

Derived

Reading a byte from SRAM costs roughly two orders of magnitude more energy than the multiply that consumes it, so the operand you pin in place should be the one you would otherwise re-fetch most often. Weight-stationary fetches each weight once per tile and reuses it M times.

DataflowStays putBest whenFor us
Weight-stationaryweights in PE regsweights reused over many samplesMandated. Also the simplest control: load, stream, drain.
Output-stationaryaccumulator in PEdeep reductions, few outputsNeeds two operand streams into the array at once. Twice the input bandwidth on a port we already contend for.
Row-stationarya mix (Eyeriss)convolution with heavy filter reuseControl complexity far beyond a first tapeout.

3.3 Accumulator width

This is where student NPUs quietly produce wrong answers, so the derivation is spelled out.

Derived

A signed INT8 lives in [−128, +127]. The extreme products are:

max = (−128) × (−128) = +16 384 = 214 min = (−128) × (+127) = −16 256

A signed value of +214 needs 16 bits, not 15 — the positive range of a 15-bit signed number stops at 16 383. Summing K such products, the exact requirement is:

bits = 1 + ⌈ log₂( K · 214 + 1 ) ⌉

and the safe rule of thumb, never wrong and occasionally one bit generous, is 16 + ⌈log₂K⌉.

CaseKWorst magnitudeBitsHeadroom in 32b
One array pass, signed465 5361814 bits
Saturation test, unsigned4260 1001814 bits
784-wide layer accumulated in HW78412 845 056257 bits
Overflow point of INT32131 0722 147 483 64832—

K = 4 sits exactly on a power of two, which is where sloppy formulas break: 4 × 214 is 216 exactly, one more than a 17-bit signed value can hold. If anyone ever proposes narrowing the psum bus to save vertical routing, this is the number they must not get wrong.

Decided

32-bit partial sums throughout. 18 bits would cover a single pass, but 32 makes the accumulator un-overflowable for every layer this chip will run, matches the SRAM word width so INT32 output needs no packing logic, and removes a whole class of bug from the K-tiling path.

The cost is real and we should own it: 32 bits between every vertically adjacent PE is 4 columns × 4 links × 32 = 512 wires of vertical routing inside a small macro, plus a 32-bit-wide de-skew bank. §12 comes back to this.

Openunassigned · before PE RTL

Signed or unsigned INT8? The brief's saturation test multiplies 0xFF by 0xFF and expects no wrap. If the PE treats 0xFF as signed −1, that test computes 4 and passes for entirely the wrong reason — it would pass on a 4-bit accumulator.

Our recommendation to the instructor: signed by default, since real quantised networks use signed symmetric weights, with a mode bit so we can run the saturation test unsigned as written. Needs a decision before anyone writes the multiplier.

04The array

4.1 Inside one PE

Decided

Three registers, one multiplier, one adder. No state machine, no counters, no knowledge of what a matrix is.

                w_in 8b            psum_in 32b
                   |                    |
 a_in 8b ----+-----|--------------------|-----> [a_reg] ---> a_out 8b
             |     v                    |
             |  [w_reg]  (en = w_load)  |
             |     |                    |
             +--> [ x ] 8x8 -> 16b      |
                    |  sign-extend      |
                    +------> [ + ] 32b <+
                               |
                          [psum_reg] (en)
                               |
                    w_out 8b   +--------> psum_out 32b

On every rising edge where en is high:

a_reg    <= a_in
psum_reg <= psum_in + (a_in * w_reg)
w_reg    <= w_in               only while w_load = 1

a_out = a_reg    psum_out = psum_reg    w_out = w_reg
Derived

Two details that are easy to get wrong and expensive to find later.

The multiplier reads a_in, not a_reg. The activation is consumed and forwarded on the same edge. That is what makes one hop cost exactly one clock, which is what the whole timing contract rests on. Multiplying the registered copy inserts an extra cycle per column and silently invalidates §4.3.

The red path is the entire STA story. Clock-to-Q, an 8×8 multiply, a 32-bit add, and setup, between two flip-flops — repeated sixteen times in a congested region. §12.2.

4.2 Why the data must arrive crooked

Derived

Look at column j. The partial sum for C[m][j] is born at the top and falls one PE per cycle, collecting one product per row:

PE(0,j):  0 + A[m][0]*B[0][j]
PE(1,j):    + A[m][1]*B[1][j]
PE(2,j):    + A[m][2]*B[2][j]
PE(3,j):    + A[m][3]*B[3][j]  ->  C[m][j]

The falling sum reaches row 1 exactly one cycle after row 0. So A[m][1] must reach row 1 exactly one cycle after A[m][0] reached row 0.

Stated as a constraint: A[m][k] enters at PE(k,0) and needs j cycles to travel to PE(k,j). The partial sum starts at PE(0,j) at time t₀ and needs k cycles to fall to row k. For them to meet:

entry(A[m][k]) + j = t₀(m,j) + k   for all k, j ⇒ entry(A[m][k]) = m + k   and   t₀(m,j) = m + j

Row k must be delayed by exactly k cycles. That is the skew, and it is the only solution — not a design choice.

Feed a whole activation row in simultaneously and the sum meets three values that are one, two and three cycles early. Every answer is wrong, and painfully, every answer still looks plausible.

Decided

Delay lines of depth 0, 1, 2, 3 on the west edge. Six flip-flop stages of 8 bits = 48 flops total. That is the entire cost of correct alignment, and it is the cheapest thing in the design.

row 0  ----------------------->        t=  0  1  2  3  4  5  6
row 1  --------[D]------------>   row0   A00 A10 A20 A30
row 2  -----[D][D]------------>   row1       A01 A11 A21 A31
row 3  --[D][D][D]------------>   row2           A02 A12 A22 A32

Latency is fixed; throughput is what you optimise. Seven cycles is the pipeline depth, four rows down plus three columns across. It never changes. What changes with M is how much useful work amortises it — see §08.2, where this turns out to be the dominant fact about the whole design.

4.4 The de-skew bank

Derived

Results emerge on the same diagonal they went in on. Column j finishes j cycles after column 0, so column j needs a delay of (3 − j) to straighten the output back into rows.

Cost: 4 lanes × up to 3 stages × 32 bits = 192 flip-flops. The single largest register block outside the array. It is expensive precisely because we chose 32-bit accumulators.

Decided

Build it. The alternative is to skew the write addresses instead and save the 192 flops. That works, but the output words leave out of order, which makes the write-back FSM harder, breaks any chance of burst behaviour on a contended bus, and makes the golden-model comparison in the testbench substantially fussier.

Without de-skew, one output row costs four separate SRAM writes instead of one — four times the write traffic on the port we are already fighting the CPU for. Spend the flops.

Derived

Note this bank is not in the brief's five subblocks. It is not scope creep: it is the unavoidable consequence of the skewing the brief does require. Worth saying explicitly in the design review, because a reviewer counting blocks will ask.

B1 — APB slave and CSR file

Derived

APB is deliberately the simplest bus in AMBA. Two phases: SETUP (psel=1, penable=0) then ACCESS (psel=1, penable=1). The slave completes by driving pready=1 during ACCESS. No bursts, no IDs, no out-of-order.

Three jobs: decode paddr[7:2]; hold configuration; turn a bus write into an event.

Decided
  • pready tied high. A configuration register has no reason to stall a CPU, and §02 note 3 means the master might be the SPI debug path, which we must never hang.
  • Unmapped offsets read as zero and ignore writes. Never stall them — a stray pointer would hang the whole SoC.
  • NPU_CTRL[0] START is write-1-to-pulse and reads back 0. As a level it would restart the FSM forever, and the sample firmware never writes it back to zero.
  • NPU_STATUS writes are silently discarded. It is R/O in the spec; a generic register array would let the CPU clobber its own DONE bit.
Openunassigned · week 2

What exactly does NPU_CTRL[1] RESET clear? FSM state and buffers, certainly. The configuration registers too? If it does, firmware must reprogram after every reset, which is probably not intended. We need to pick one and document it in the header file.

B3 — DMA master engine

Derived

The bandwidth arithmetic is the whole point of this block. The array consumes four INT8 activations per cycle, one per row. That is exactly 4 bytes, exactly one 32-bit SRAM word, per cycle. The interface can supply at best one word per cycle.

So at full speed the uNPU wants 100% of the SRAM port, and it is sharing that port with a cacheless PicoRV32 fetching an instruction almost every cycle. We will never get 100%. Every arbitration loss becomes a bubble, and bubbles in a systolic array propagate.

arithmetic intensity = 2·M·K·N ops / (M·K + K·N + M·N) bytes for M=K=N=4: 128 ops per 48 bytes — firmly memory-bound

That ratio improves linearly with M, which is the same conclusion §08.2 reaches from the latency side. Two independent arguments for batching.

Decided

Prefetch a whole tile into B5 while the previous tile computes, then stream from the buffer at a guaranteed full rate. This is the only structure that decouples a fixed-latency array from a contended bus.

B4 — Weight buffer and column loader

Derived

Weights arrive as four 32-bit words and must end up in sixteen different PEs.

MethodWiring costCyclesVerdict
Parallel broadcast, dedicated 8-bit bus per PE128 wires fanning across the array1Routing nightmare in a small congested macro
Shift-down chain, reusing vertical neighbours4 buses of 8 bits4Use this
Decided

Shift-down chain, and load in reverse row order — row 3's weights pushed first, so after four shifts every value has settled in its intended row. This off-by-one is the most common bug in a first systolic array. Write it as a testbench assertion before writing the loader.

Double-buffer the weight bank so the next tile's weights load while the current one streams. On the utilisation table in §08.2 this is worth several points and costs 16 bytes of registers.

B5 — Activation buffer

Decided

A register-file-based buffer rather than a second SRAM macro. Adding an SRAM macro to a 700 µm floorplan costs more in placement grief than it saves in area at this size.

Size is a live question. 64 × 32 bits (256 B) gives comfortable ping-pong headroom but costs roughly 2 000 flops, which would dominate our non-array flop count and change both the scan chain length and the CTS load. 32 × 32 bits halves that. We are provisionally taking 32 × 32 bits and will revisit after the first synthesis run tells us what we can afford.

Whatever we pick, the number must be the same here and in the §12.1 area table. A discrepancy between those two is exactly the kind of thing that makes an area estimate useless.

B6 / B8 — Skew and de-skew banks

Decided

Implement as shift registers with an enable, not as FIFO IP. There is no back-pressure inside the array, so nothing can ever be full or empty; pointers and flags would be dead logic and a source of bugs.

Write one parameterised module, instantiate four times with DEPTH = i. Handle DEPTH = 0 explicitly — it is a wire, and a parameterised loop that accidentally infers one register on row 0 shifts the entire wavefront and fails the identity test.

B7 — Grid assembly

Decided

Nested generate loops, sixteen instances. Edge rules:

  • Column 0's a_in comes from the skew bank.
  • Row 0's psum_in is a mux: zero normally, and the stored running sum when the accumulate mode of §08.1 is active. Making it a mux from the start costs almost nothing and buys bias-add for free later.
  • Interior: a_out → a_in eastward, psum_out → psum_in southward.
  • Row 3's psum_out goes to B8.
  • The east edge's a_out goes nowhere and will be optimised away. That is correct and expected — do not let it worry anyone reading the synthesis log.

B10 — Reset, clock, DFT

Decided

One clock domain, so no CDC anywhere in this macro. Say so explicitly in the design review; it is a genuine simplification and reviewers will otherwise ask.

Reset is asynchronous-assert, synchronous-de-assert through a two-flop synchroniser, to avoid a metastable release across a few thousand flops.

Clock-gate the array enable. Sixteen multipliers toggling while the sequencer is idle is pure wasted power.

Openunassigned · before port list freeze

Scan chain. There are no scan ports in the given port list and DFT is not mentioned anywhere in the course brief. But a macro that cannot be scan-tested cannot be tested on a tester, and Project 1's BIST FSM implies somebody expects post-fabrication structural test.

We should request scan_en, scan_in, scan_out. Adding pins after the macro is hardened means re-hardening it, so this has to be settled before floorplan, not after. Bundle it with the interrupt question (§13 item 8).

06Datapath

Derived

Follow one 32-bit word from SRAM to SRAM and you have seen the whole arithmetic pipeline. It changes width five times.

SRAM word -> unpack -> skew -> 16x MAC array -> de-skew -> pack -> SRAM word
   32b       4 x 8b   4 x 8b   8x8->16, +32b    4 x 32b   32b       32b
                                    ^
                        weights held stationary, 16 x 8b

one 32-bit SRAM word feeds exactly one cycle of the array:
the design is bandwidth-balanced by construction

The only place the data gets wider is the accumulator, and it stays wide until it is written out. The 4×32-bit region between the array and the packer is where most of our flip-flops and most of our vertical routing congestion live.

The stall rule

Decided

The array is a fixed-latency clockwork; the world at its edges is not. There is exactly one safe way to reconcile them.

When anything stalls, the skew FIFOs, all sixteen PEs, and the de-skew FIFOs freeze on the same clock edge. One global array_en feeds the enable of every register in the compute pipeline. Freeze part of it and the wavefront shears: results stay plausible and become wrong.

Never build per-block flow control inside the array. If someone proposes it in review, this is the paragraph to point at.

To make stalls rare rather than merely safe: a two-entry skid buffer in B9, plus enough headroom in B5 that a read stall never reaches the array. Then array_en is high essentially all the time and we get to reason about the array as if the bus were perfect.

07Control path

Decided

Three FSMs, not one. One sequencer owning the job, one bus FSM owning the APB port, one handshake FSM owning the memory port. They communicate only through request/done pairs.

The reason is not tidiness. The APB FSM must answer the bus in a fixed two-cycle pattern regardless of what compute is doing — if the CPU polls NPU_STATUS mid-stream and our slave stalls, we have coupled the CPU's execution to our pipeline. Similarly the DMA FSM must be free to sit waiting on the arbiter without freezing the sequencer's counters.

Three small machines with req/done between them are individually provable. One machine with forty states is not.

Decided

Moore machine: outputs depend only on state, never combinationally on inputs. Costs a cycle here and there, buys clean timing and a readable waveform.

7.2 DMA handshake FSM

Derived

Four states, and the entire subtlety is in what must stay stable.

StateOutputsTransition
D_IDLEdma_valid = 0dma_req → D_REQ; load base into address counter, length into beat counter
D_REQdma_valid = 1, addr, wstrbdma_ready → D_ACK; otherwise hold everything stable and stay

The address advances only on cycles where dma_valid and dma_ready are both high. Incrementing on valid alone silently skips words and is the classic DMA bug. It produces a result that is wrong in a way no waveform makes obvious.

Decided

Build the four-state version first and prove it correct. A tuned version collapses D_REQ and D_ACK so the next address is presented on the same cycle a beat is accepted, sustaining one word per clock — but that version is much harder to debug while the arbiter is also under test. Optimise after the identity test passes, not before.

08Tiling and the utilisation problem

Openunassigned · week 1, blocking

Is tiling in scope at all? MATRIX_CFG gives 16 bits each to Rows and Columns, which is far more than a 4×4 array needs and strongly suggests matrices larger than 4×4 are expected. But the sample firmware writes 0x00040004 and the brief's required tests are all 4×4.

This is the single biggest scope question in the project. Full tiling means a substantially larger sequencer, K-accumulation storage, and a much bigger verification matrix. Get an answer in week one.

The rest of this section assumes the answer is yes, because it is cheaper to design for and not build than to retrofit.

Derived

An M×K by K×N multiply becomes ⌈M/4⌉ × ⌈K/4⌉ × ⌈N/4⌉ passes. The loop nesting order decides what gets reloaded.

Order (outer → inner)Weight reloadsNeeds psum storageVerdict

m, n, kevery passno — K completes in one goSimplest control, worst weight traffic. Fine for bring-up
m, k, nevery passyesWorst of both. Avoid

8.1 Accumulating across K tiles

Decided

When K > 4 one pass produces only part of each output. Read-modify-write in SRAM would triple memory traffic on a port we are already fighting for. Instead, mux row 0's psum_in: zero for the first K-tile, the stored running sum for every subsequent one, and a bias value if we want bias-add. No extra adder, no extra cycles, no extra memory traffic.

Derived

The injected sum must itself be skewed. From §4.3, the partial sum for C[m][j] must be present at PE(0,j) at cycle m + j, not cycle m. So the accumulator read path needs its own staircase of delays 0/1/2/3, 32 bits wide — another 192 flops, the same cost as the de-skew bank.

This is easy to miss and it is exactly the class of alignment bug §00 warns about. The idea survives; it is not free.

09Quantisation and MNIST

Spec

Signoff requires "hardware/software co-simulation utilizing RISC-V C-firmware to execute live inference on a quantized MNIST digit". The uNPU block diagram labels the software "CNN RISC-V Firmware".

Openunassigned · before firmware

CNN or MLP? The block diagram says CNN; the brief text says only "quantized MNIST digit". These imply very different firmware. A convolution needs an im2col transform on the CPU to become a matrix multiply, which is real work and real SRAM. A small fully-connected net is far simpler and maps directly.

Our preference is a small MLP for the signoff test, with the argument that the array does GEMM either way and im2col is a firmware concern. Needs agreement.

9.1 The quantisation model

Derived

Every real tensor is an INT8, a float scale and an integer zero point: r = S(q − Z). Substituted into a dot product:

r_out = Sa·Sw · [ Σaq·wq − ZwΣaq − ZaΣwq + K·Za·Zw ]

Four terms, only the first of which is a matrix multiply. Quantising weights symmetrically forces Zw = 0 and kills two terms outright, leaving:

Σaq·wq − Za · ( Σk wq[k][j] )

The second term is a per-output-column constant. Fold it into the bias, computed once in C when the model is prepared.

Decided

Symmetric weights, bias folding, all zero-point bookkeeping in software. The array then computes a plain integer dot product and nothing else. Our sixteen PEs stay dumb, which is exactly what we want them to be.

9.2 Requantisation

Derived

The accumulator holds the real value scaled by Sa·Sw. To store it as the next layer's INT8 you multiply by M = Sa·Sw/Sy, always in (0,1). Hardware cannot multiply by a float, so write M as fixed point:

M = M₀ · 2−n, with M₀ ∈ [0.5, 1) as a 32-bit integer yq = clamp( ((acc · M₀) >>> (31 + n)) + Zy, −128, 127 )

The shift is a rounding shift: add 2shift−1 before shifting. ReLU is free — applied before requantisation it just changes the clamp's lower bound from −128 to Zy. One mux.

Note this runs once per output, not once per MAC, so it can be slow multi-cycle logic if that ever helps timing.

Decided

Per §05, this lives in firmware for v1. The arithmetic above is recorded here so that if we do move it into hardware, we are implementing a known-correct algorithm rather than inventing one under schedule pressure.

9.3 The 32 KB reality check

Derived

A textbook 784→64→10 MNIST classifier needs 784×64 = 50 176 INT8 weights for the first layer alone. That is 49 KB. The whole SoC has 32 KB of SRAM, shared with firmware .text, .data, stack, and our activation and output buffers.

The reference network does not fit. This is a hard constraint, not a tuning issue.

Openunassigned · before firmware and memory map

Which way out do we take? Options, in increasing order of effort:

  • Shrink the first layer. 784→16 is 12.5 KB and still reaches roughly 92% on MNIST.
  • Downsample the image to 14×14 = 196 inputs. 196×64 is also 12.5 KB.
  • Stream weights from off-chip through Project 1's SPI controller. A system-level conversation, not a decision we can make alone.

This changes the memory map, so it must be settled before §10.2 is finalised.

Decided

#define NPU_CTRL   (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x00))
#define NPU_STATUS (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x04))
#define NPU_SRC    (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x08))
#define NPU_DST    (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x0C))
#define NPU_CFG    (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x10))
#define NPU_ACT    (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x14))
#define NPU_CFG2   (*(volatile uint32_t*)(NPU_BASE+0x18))

#define CTRL_START (1u<<0)
#define CTRL_ACCUM (1u<<3)
#define ST_DONE    (1u<<0)
#define ST_ERROR   (1u<<2)

int npu_gemm(const int8_t *w, const int8_t *a, void *out,
             uint16_t M, uint16_t K, uint16_t N, int accumulate)
{
    NPU_SRC  = (uint32_t)w;
    NPU_ACT  = (uint32_t)a;
    NPU_DST  = (uint32_t)out;
    NPU_CFG  = ((uint32_t)N << 16) | M;
    NPU_CFG2 = (1u<<16) | (uint32_t)K;        /* SIGNED, K */
    NPU_CTRL = CTRL_START | (accumulate ? CTRL_ACCUM : 0);

    while (!(NPU_STATUS & (ST_DONE | ST_ERROR)))
        ;
    return (NPU_STATUS & ST_ERROR) ? -1 : 0;
}

Three things that matter in real firmware. Use volatile or the compiler hoists the status read out of the loop and the CPU hangs. Poll DONE and ERROR, or an error becomes an infinite loop in silicon — precisely the failure Project 1's SPI backdoor exists to rescue us from. And note the brief's sample never writes DMA_DST_ADDR; we require it.

11Verification

Decided

Build order matters more than coverage numbers early on. This list is in the order we will actually do it, not in order of importance.

  1. PE, directed. Twenty vectors on one PE: max positive, max negative, zero weight, weight load while computing (must not disturb the accumulation). Five minutes of simulation, saves days.
  2. Skew and de-skew, directed. Push a known ramp through and assert each lane's delay is exactly 0/1/2/3. Assert all four lanes freeze together when array_en drops.
  3. Grid alone, no DMA, no APB. Testbench forces weights, injects activations by hand, runs the identity test. This is the milestone that proves the timing contract.
  4. Array vs golden model. A behavioural reference computing A×B directly. A thousand random INT8 pairs compared element by element. Where the bugs the directed tests miss actually live.
  5. Randomised bus back-pressure. Drive dma_ready from a random generator at 30–70% duty. The highest-value test after identity, because a systolic array that only works with a perfect memory works on your bench and fails in the SoC — and §02 says our memory is definitely not perfect.
  6. APB protocol. Every register including reserved holes; back-to-back transfers; a read during BUSY; START while BUSY (must set ERR_CODE, not corrupt the running job); SOFT_RST mid-stream (must return cleanly to IDLE); very slow transfers, to emulate the SPI debug master.
  7. Tiling. Non-square, non-multiple-of-four shapes. 7×5 by 5×3 is a good case because every dimension needs padding. Check padded lanes contribute zero and do not leak.
  8. Assertions, running always. dma_valid stable until ready; dma_addr[1:0] == 0; DMA addresses inside SRAM range; DONE never rises before the last write-back is accepted; no X propagation into psum.
  9. Coverage. All sequencer states; every ERR_CODE; both sign modes; at least one job with M = 1 and one with M > 64.
  10. HW/SW co-simulation. Compiled RISC-V firmware running MNIST inference against the SoC. The signoff test, and the one that finds memory-map disagreements with Project 1.
  11. Gate-level with SDF. Re-run identity and saturation on the netlist with back-annotated timing. Catches hold violations and X-through-reset problems RTL simulation cannot see.

What the two required tests actually prove

Derived

Identity is an alignment test wearing a math test's clothes. Because the correct answer is the input unchanged, any misalignment — a one-cycle skew error, a transposed weight load, a de-skew off by one — shows up as a visibly shifted or permuted matrix. It is the single most informative test in the plan.

Run it in four variants: A×I, I×B, a transposed identity, and a permutation matrix. The permutation is the best of the four, because a symmetric identity can hide errors that a rearrangement exposes.

Saturation proves accumulator width, sign extension of the 16-bit product into 32 bits, and clamp-versus-wrap. Note from §3.3 that it only proves anything if the sign convention is settled first.

Decided

A debugging habit to adopt from day one. Dump every PE's psum_reg every cycle to a file and diff against a spreadsheet of the expected wavefront built from the §4.3 table. When something misaligns, the diff names the first cycle and the first PE that disagree. That pins the bug in minutes instead of hours of waveform staring.

The pipelining trap. Pipelining only the partial-sum path looks correct in a block diagram and produces wrong answers in simulation. The array's correctness rests on activations and partial sums hopping at exactly the same rate.

If you register the product, you must also add a register on the activation forwarding path. Then the skew depths become 0/2/4/6, the pipeline depth doubles from 7 to 14, and every number in §4.3 changes. It is a coherent design, but it is a different design, and it cannot be retrofitted late.

12.4 Congestion, constraints, signoff

Derived

Congestion. Every vertical PE boundary carries 32 wires; the array's bottom edge carries 4 columns × 32 = 128. Keep utilisation around 65–70%, leave a routing channel below the array, and check the M3/M4 track budget against the power straps. Project 3 is fighting the same M3/M4 collision with a routing matrix instead of buses — worth comparing notes with them.

Power. Sixteen multipliers switching every cycle is a genuine dynamic-IR concern in a 180 nm mesh. Run dynamic IR analysis rather than trusting a static one, and gate the array clock when the sequencer is idle or waiting on DMA.

CTS. One domain, a few thousand flops. Nothing exotic, but balance skew across the array deliberately: a PE that clocks early relative to its southern neighbour eats directly into the psum path's setup margin.

Decided

SDC essentials. create_clock on clk; set_clock_uncertainty covering CTS skew and jitter; input and output delays on every APB and DMA pin negotiated with Project 1 rather than guessed, since guessing these is how hierarchical STA falls apart at the top level; set_case_analysis on scan_en = 0 for functional runs if we get scan.

On reset: do not blanket set_false_path -from rst_n. The asynchronous assertion path is untimed, but the synchronised de-assertion fans out to every flop in the macro and must be timed, or recovery and removal violations are hidden rather than fixed.

15Team and work split

Five people, roughly 10 hours each per week, both front-end and back-end. The split below is built around one fact that is easy to miss until it hurts.

Derived

The two halves of this project have opposite shapes.

The front end parallelises well. The PE, the FIFO banks, the DMA, the APB wrapper and the sequencer have clean interfaces and can genuinely be written by five different people at the same time. Five people is close to the right number.

The back end does not parallelise. Floorplan, PDN, placement, CTS, routing, STA and physical verification are stages of one database, largely sequential. Three people is about the practical maximum and the third is often waiting. You cannot rescue a late back end by adding bodies to it.

So the schedule in §16 front-loads five-way parallel work and tapers to three-way sequential work, with the other two on firmware, gate-level simulation and the report. Plan for that shape rather than discovering it.

15.3 Rules that prevent the expensive failures

Decided
  • One person owns the timing contract. The §4.3 table spans every block, and a disagreement about it produces plausible wrong answers rather than obvious breakage. Any change to those four equations goes through A and gets a line in §14.
  • Nobody merges their own untested RTL. A module lands when its unit test passes, not when it compiles. This is cheap now and unaffordable in week 6.
  • Two people can drive P&R, even if only one normally does. Everything after week 5 is sequential and owned by three people. If one has exams or hits a licence problem there is no way to parallelise around them. Twenty minutes of shoulder-surfing in week 3 is the insurance.
  • Interfaces are agreed before implementation. The internal signal lists in §02 are the contract between owners. Change one and tell the person on the other side the same day.
  • Every open question in §13 has a named owner by end of week 1. Unowned questions do not get answered; they get discovered in week 6.

15.4 Weekly cadence

Decided

Ten hours a week is roughly two working sessions. Suggested shape:

  • One long block (5–6 hrs) for real work — RTL, debugging, a P&R run. Tool work in particular is badly served by short sessions; you spend the first hour reloading context.
  • One shorter block (3–4 hrs) for unit tests, review and integration.
  • One 30-minute all-hands, same time every week. Three questions only: what landed, what is blocked, what changed in §13 or §14. Not a status theatre.

Whoever runs the all-hands updates this notebook in the same sitting. A decision that is agreed verbally and never written down will be re-litigated in week 6.

16Eight-week plan

Derived

The budget. 5 people × 10 hours × 8 weeks = 400 person-hours. That is the entire project: RTL, verification, firmware, synthesis, P&R, STA, DRC/LVS and the report.

It is achievable, but only with scope frozen early and the back end started in week 2. There is no version of this schedule where six weeks of RTL leaves enough time to harden a macro.

16.1 Shape of the eight weeks

16.2 The structural idea: harden early, on purpose, knowing it is wrong

Decided

In week 3, take whatever RTL exists — even just the PE array with no DMA — and push it all the way through Genus, IC Compiler and a DRC run. Throw the result away. Keep the scripts, the library setup, the SDC skeleton and the real critical-path number.

That pass costs roughly 30 person-hours and buys three things you would otherwise get in week 7, when it is too late to act on any of them:

  • Whether the PE closes timing, which decides the pipelining question in §12.2 — and that decision changes the skew depths and every number in §4.3.
  • Whether the §12.1 area estimate is anywhere near right.
  • A working cross-vendor tool flow, which is the thing that actually eats weeks.

Rejected alternative: finish the RTL first and harden once, properly. It is tidier and it is how teams run out of time. A discarded trial pass is the cheapest de-risking available on this project.

16.6 What to cut, in order

Decided

Deciding this order now, calmly, is much better than deciding it in week 6 under pressure. Cut from the top the moment you are behind.

  1. Tiling beyond 4×4 (Q2). Worth 60–80 hours on its own. If the instructor confirms it is not required, buffer goes from 4% to roughly 20% and this project becomes comfortable. Ask this first.
  2. Weight double-buffering. Costs utilisation, not correctness.
  3. K-accumulation mode. Keep the mux on row 0's psum_in in the RTL, do not verify the mode.
  4. Packed INT8 output. Ship INT32 only.
  5. Clock gating and the interrupt.

Never cut: the identity and saturation tests, the de-skew bank, randomised back-pressure testing, or DRC/LVS. The first three are correctness and the last is a deliverable.

16.7 The two risks worth naming

Derived

Project 1 dependency. MNIST co-simulation needs a working SoC that another team is building on the same schedule. That is outside your control and it is your largest schedule risk.

Mitigation, week 3, ~15 hours: build your own mini-SoC testbench. PicoRV32 is open-source Verilog; add a behavioural SRAM model and a trivial APB bridge you write yourself. Then your co-simulation deliverable depends on nobody. Integrating with the real Project 1 SoC afterwards becomes a bonus rather than a blocker.

Derived

Sequential back end, three owners. Everything after week 5 is one database and three people. Illness, exams or a tool licence problem in that window cannot be parallelised around.

Mitigation: the shoulder-surfing rule in §15.3. Make sure at least two people can drive the P&R flow before week 5, even if only one normally does.
